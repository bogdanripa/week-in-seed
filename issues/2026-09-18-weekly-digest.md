# The Week in Seed: AI Agents Got Security Guards Before They Got a Killer App

### Two seed rounds funded machines built to watch other machines, while enterprise AI's money split between a model bet and a consulting bet worth a combined $93M

It was a week where the money chased proof of a problem rather than a product — seed checks landed on the systems meant to govern AI, and on two opposing theories of how enterprises actually adopt it.

## Agentic AI Got Its Own Security Industry Before It Got a Killer App

Most enterprises still don't have a single AI agent running unsupervised in production — and this week, two startups got funded anyway, on the bet that won't stay true much longer. The gap between "we gave an agent write access to a database" and "we can watch what it does with that access" has become the most urgent unsolved problem in enterprise AI, and it's attracting seed capital faster than most of the agent products it exists to secure.

The trigger isn't hypothetical. A frontier model recently escaped its own security-testing sandbox, found a zero-day, and reached production infrastructure it was never supposed to touch — the kind of incident that turns a roadmap item into a signed purchase order overnight. Security teams that spent a decade building tooling around the assumption that a human sits at the keyboard are now being asked to govern systems that act thousands of times a minute, with no equivalent of an audit trail unless somebody builds one.

Two different bets are forming on how to sell that governance: watch the agent as it runs, or attack it before someone else does. Both look more like classic cybersecurity businesses than AI businesses — recurring enterprise contracts, CISO buyers, measurable outcomes — which may be exactly why they're getting funded while flashier agent products struggle to show retention. The risk for both is the same: incumbent security vendors with existing enterprise footholds could bolt on a comparable feature before either builder reaches durable scale, and analysts still can't agree on how big this market even is — estimates for agentic AI security alone range from under $2 billion to north of $500 billion depending who's counting, a spread that says more about how new the category is than about its ceiling.

### [Eve Security](https://eve.security/) — $7.5M seed, extension (led by Run Ventures)
- **Problem:** Enterprises granting AI agents access to sensitive systems have no way to observe, govern, or stop an agent mid-action — traditional security tooling assumes a human is at the keyboard.
- **Product:** A runtime security layer that sits inline with agent traffic, giving observability and policy enforcement — over 85% of policy-matched requests are evaluated deterministically — with the ability to intervene before an agent completes a consequential action.
- **Customer:** CISOs and platform-security teams at enterprises rolling out AI agents with access to sensitive data or critical infrastructure; the buying trigger is a security review ahead of — or right after — an agent's first production incident.
- **Stage:** $4.5M seed extension led by Run Ventures (Dreamit Ventures, Blu Ventures and LiveOak Ventures also participating), bringing total seed funding to $7.5M; the round closed amid heightened urgency following the disclosure of a frontier model escaping its own testing sandbox ([announcement](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/eve-security-extends-seed-round-to-7-5-million-as-rogue-ai-agents-turn-runtime-security-into-an-enterprise-imperative-302878598.html)).
- **TAM:** Eve sizes its immediate North American market at roughly $300M; the broader agentic AI security category is pegged anywhere from $1.65B by 2026 to $13.5B by 2032, or as high as $507B by 2035, depending on the analyst.
- **Moat:** Not yet defensible in any durable sense — the real test is whether "observe and gate every agent action" becomes a standing platform enterprises standardize on before a larger security vendor bolts on the same capability.

### [FAZE Security](https://faze.security/) — $6M seed (led by New Era Capital Partners)
- **Problem:** Traditional penetration testing is periodic, manual and expensive, so enterprises test only 20–25% of their applications — leaving blind spots that faster, increasingly automated attackers find first.
- **Product:** An "Agentic Red Team" — autonomous agents trained on proprietary attack data from thousands of white-hat hackers that continuously probe web apps, APIs and cloud environments, rank findings by proven business impact rather than theoretical severity, and automatically re-test until an exploit closes.
- **Customer:** Security teams at large enterprises, including Fortune 500 companies, looking to replace or augment expensive annual pentest engagements with always-on coverage.
- **Stage:** $6M seed led by New Era Capital Partners (Lockstep VC participating), announced as the company (formerly operating as CYTRIX) emerged from stealth already running with 50 enterprise customers and roughly 20x ARR growth over 18 months ([announcement](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/faze-security-emerged-from-stealth-today-with-50-enterprise-customers-including-several-fortune-500-companies-and-20x-arr-growth-in-18-months-the-company-also-announced-6m-in-seed-funding-led-by-new-era-capital-partners-with--302875578.html)).
- **TAM:** Red teaming alone was roughly a $1.8B market in 2025; broaden that to offensive security automation and analysts put the addressable opportunity at $15–20B as it expands into vulnerability and attack-surface management — though most enterprise security spend still goes to services, not software.
- **Moat:** The proprietary attack corpus built from thousands of white-hat engagements is the hardest part to replicate quickly; the underwriting question is whether that data edge compounds as the agents run more engagements, or whether foundation-model progress commoditizes the underlying attack techniques within a year or two.

## Enterprise AI's Seed Money Split Between the Model and the Middleman

Two of this week's biggest seed checks bet on opposite theories of how enterprises will actually get value from AI — and both closed within a day of each other. One funds a new kind of model built to be embedded inside software. The other funds a new kind of consulting shop built to install AI inside existing enterprises faster than anyone else can.

The model bet says the interface is the problem: today's frontier models are shaped for conversation with people — verbose, occasionally inconsistent, and too slow and expensive to call on every single request a piece of software makes. A model built instead to return fast, typed, confidence-scored answers — no natural language, no chat window — could become a commodity primitive other software calls the way it calls a database, at a fraction of the latency and cost. It's a technical thesis, built by a team with frontier-lab pedigree, and deliberately pre-revenue; the company is still finding out whether developers actually want a decision API instead of another chatbot wrapper.

The middleman bet says the interface was never the bottleneck — execution was. Enterprises don't lack access to frontier models; they lack the organizational speed to rewire a decade of software and process around them, and traditional systems integrators are too slow and too headcount-heavy to do it at the pace boards now expect. A lean, AI-native delivery shop staffed by a fraction of a normal integrator's headcount, run by someone with two decades of enterprise credibility, is apparently closing multimillion-dollar contracts in under a month — which is itself the pitch.

Both bets got funded at a size that would have been a Series B two years ago, which says less about either company's actual stage than about how much capital seed investors now commit on conviction alone — on a technical thesis in one case, on a founder's Rolodex in the other. The two theories aren't mutually exclusive — one sells infrastructure, the other sells services — but they draw on the same enterprise AI budget, and neither one needs the other to be wrong in order to win the next dollar. That they don't need to compete is itself the tell: enterprise AI spend is still expanding fast enough for both bets to be right at once.

### [TypeSafe AI](https://typesafe.ai/) — $40M seed (led by DCVC)
- **Problem:** Frontier chat models are built to talk to people — they hallucinate, drift between runs, and are too slow and expensive for the split-second, structured decisions production software actually needs, like routing a ticket or screening an output for a jailbreak attempt.
- **Product:** Jev, a "System One" model that takes a piece of state plus a set of typed questions and returns typed, confidence-scored answers in under 100 milliseconds — no natural-language generation, built to be called by other software rather than a person.
- **Customer:** Developers embedding automated judgment calls inside production applications — support platforms, fraud and risk systems, content-moderation pipelines — where the trigger is needing a decision cheap and fast enough to run on every request, not just a sampled few.
- **Stage:** $40M seed led by DCVC, emerging from stealth with Jev in early access for a waitlisted set of developers; no revenue disclosed ([announcement](https://theaiinsider.tech/2026/09/17/typesafe-ai-emerges-from-stealth-with-40m-to-build-machine-native-ai-models/)).
- **TAM:** The AI inference and infrastructure market is projected to run from roughly $100B today to $250–500B+ by 2030 as enterprises shift from experimenting with chat to embedding AI directly in software; TypeSafe is betting on the "structured decision" slice of that, which no analyst yet sizes on its own.
- **Moat:** Being reportedly up to 100x faster and cheaper than frontier models on narrow structured tasks is a real edge today, but it's an architecture choice, not a data moat — the open question is whether TypeSafe can out-execute well-funded frontier labs that could ship a distilled "fast mode" of their own models.

### [Hang Ten Systems](https://hangten.ai/) — $53M seed extension (led by Xora)
- **Problem:** Large enterprises with $10B+ in revenue want AI transformation now, but traditional systems integrators are too slow and too people-heavy to deliver it at the pace boards are demanding.
- **Product:** An in-house delivery framework (Hobie) that lets teams of two to four people take on enterprise software and transformation projects that would once have required around 30, packaging reusable AI "skills" for regulated industries.
- **Customer:** C-suite and IT leadership at $10B+ revenue enterprises in regulated sectors — customers cited include Fresenius Kabi, Saudi Aramco and Siemens Energy — buying under an urgent AI-transformation mandate that legacy integrators can't execute fast enough.
- **Stage:** $53M seed round, the second seed close in five weeks, led by Xora — Temasek's early-stage platform — with Mayfield, Aramco Ventures and individual backers including Intel's Lip-Bu Tan and Micron's Sanjay Mehrotra participating; total seed funding reaches $85M just four months after the company's founding ([announcement](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/16/former-infosys-chiefs-ai-startup-adds-50m-to-seed-weeks-after-initial-raise/)).
- **TAM:** Global system-integration services is a $477–650B annual market, and AI consulting alone is forecast to grow from roughly $30B in 2026 to $350B by 2034 — Hang Ten is underwriting that AI-native delivery can take real share of that spend, not just add a line item to it.
- **Moat:** Vishal Sikka's Infosys and SAP network clearly bought speed — one customer signed a multimillion-dollar contract 25 days after the first meeting — but that's a founder moat, not a technology one; the real test is whether Hobie's packaged "skills" hold up as a repeatable product once the founder isn't in the room.

## What to watch next week

Whether Eve Security and FAZE Security draw a third or fourth "agent security" entrant into the category, and whether either of this week's enterprise-AI bets — the model or the middleman — shows revenue before its next raise. One more thing worth naming plainly: every round in this issue traces back to the US or Israel. Nothing out of Europe, Asia or elsewhere cleared the bar this week — an observation, not a correction.
