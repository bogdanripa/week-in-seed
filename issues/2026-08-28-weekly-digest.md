# The Week in Seed: The Robots Didn't Get the Money — the Reactor, the Router and the Simulator Did

### This week's biggest seed checks funded the power, compute-routing and training-simulation layers underneath physical AI, while two much smaller pre-seed rounds bet on rebuilding audit and portfolio management as AI-native firms from day one.

It was a week that skipped the visible product almost entirely — nearly every check, big or small, went one layer beneath whatever an end user would actually see or touch.

## Physical AI's biggest checks went to power, compute and training — not to a single robot

Two years into the humanoid-robot and embodied-AI hype cycle, this week's money didn't go to a robot company at all. It went to the three things any fleet of embodied AI systems needs before it can exist at scale: dedicated power for the data centers that train and run it, a way to route inference across whichever chips are cheapest without getting locked to one vendor, and a place to train the agents themselves that doesn't require millions of dangerous, unparallelizable real-world hours. Investors are underwriting the stack, not the demo.

That's a rational response to two things happening at once. Grid interconnection queues for new data centers already stretch years in most US markets, so anyone who can put dedicated power on-site in under two years has a real business regardless of what runs on top of it. And the current generation of embodied-AI labs has run into a data problem compute money can't solve directly: you can't parallelize a robot's real-world hours the way you parallelize GPU cycles, so the training bottleneck has shifted to whoever can build a convincing enough simulated world to substitute for them.

Two weeks ago we covered the same underlying pressure from the demand side — the heat and grid bills AI compute was running up. This week's money moved one step further upstream, to generation itself, plus the orchestration and training layers that decide how efficiently that power gets used once it's on the grid. Who wins if this holds: infrastructure vendors who can license, permit or partner their way into becoming the default layer other AI companies build on. Who's exposed: application-layer robotics and model companies that still have to solve hardware capex and real-world validation on top of whatever infrastructure they buy.

### [Apollo Atomics](https://www.apolloatomics.com/) — $31M seed (led by FCVC)
- **Problem:** AI data centers and heavy industrial users need firm, dedicated power fast, but utility-scale nuclear plants take a decade or more to permit and build, and grid interconnection queues for new capacity already stretch years in most US markets.
- **Product:** factory-built, truck-shippable pressurized-water microreactors — a 10-megawatt A-10, a 50-megawatt A-50, and a 300-megawatt A-300 — designed to be manufactured in a factory rather than built on-site, cutting the reactor's physical footprint roughly 40-fold and its deployment timeline to under two years.
- **Customer:** data center operators, utilities and industrial energy users who need permittable, dedicated power on a two-year timeline instead of the decade-plus utility-scale nuclear build cycle.
- **Stage:** $31M seed — $26M in equity led by FCVC plus $5M in equipment financing from Oak Ridge Financial — the company's first institutional raise; Apollo says it already has more than 20 gigawatts of signed letters of intent in its commercial pipeline, though those are stated interest, not binding contracts ([announcement](https://techstartups.com/2026/08/20/mit-spinoff-apollo-atomics-raises-31m-to-build-factory-made-nuclear-reactors-for-ai-data-centers/)).
- **TAM:** the broader small-modular-reactor market is forecast to reach roughly $54B by 2036 per IDTechEx, while the narrower data-center-specific SMR segment alone is projected to grow from about $38M in 2028 to $278M by 2033 — both early-stage analyst estimates for a technology that hasn't yet cleared US regulatory approval at commercial scale.
- **Moat:** an MIT nuclear-engineering pedigree and Washington policy experience on the founding team is a real head start in a licensing-gated industry, but the actual moat is whoever gets through NRC approval and stands up repeatable factory tooling first — 20 gigawatts of letters of intent is a demand signal, not proof any of it converts to a signed, financed reactor.

### [Callosum](https://www.callosum.com/) — $100M seed (led by Atomico)
- **Problem:** enterprises and governments running AI at production scale don't want to be locked into one frontier model or one chip architecture, since that concentrates cost, latency and sovereignty risk in a single vendor's hands.
- **Product:** a "programmable heterogeneity" platform that breaks AI workloads into individual tasks and routes each one to whichever model-and-chip combination is cheapest, fastest or most energy-efficient for that specific task, delivered through a Tailored Inference API with a flagship low-latency partnership with Cerebras.
- **Customer:** enterprises and national governments running AI workloads at scale who want cost and latency control without being tied to a single semiconductor or model vendor — the kind of "AI sovereignty" concern that drew the UK Sovereign AI Fund's first-ever investment.
- **Stage:** $100M seed led by Atomico, following a $10.25M seed round in February 2026 when the company emerged from stealth, bringing total raised to roughly $110M; Plural, DCVC and the UK Sovereign AI Fund also participated ([announcement](https://www.vestbee.com/insights/articles/callosum-raises-100-m)).
- **TAM:** the global AI inference market is estimated at roughly $106–118B in 2025–26, growing toward $250–310B by 2030 across several analyst forecasts — Callosum is a thin orchestration layer on top of that spend, not a claim on the whole market.
- **Moat:** routing and orchestration is a shallow enough layer that hyperscalers and chip vendors could plausibly build it in-house; Callosum's actual bet is speed to exclusive infrastructure partnerships like Cerebras and a sovereign-AI positioning that's as much political as technical, not a defensible algorithm.

### [Veeda](https://veeda.ai/) — $90M seed (co-led by Khosla Ventures and Radical Ventures)
- **Problem:** training embodied and robotic AI requires enormous amounts of real-world interaction that's slow, dangerous in edge cases, and physically impossible to parallelize the way GPU compute is.
- **Product:** multimodal "world models" that simulate physical environments in enough fidelity that robots and other embodied agents can be trained through repeated interaction in a simulated reality instead of the real one.
- **Customer:** robotics developers and manufacturers building autonomous or humanoid systems who need a safe, scalable training environment before committing to expensive real-world deployment and validation.
- **Stage:** $90M seed — the company's first financing, closed roughly three months after incorporation — co-led by Khosla Ventures and Radical Ventures, reportedly one of the largest seed rounds ever raised by a Canadian startup ([announcement](https://siliconangle.com/2026/08/19/sanja-fidlers-world-model-startup-veeda-ai-raises-90m-in-seed-funding/)).
- **TAM:** the simulation-based AI training market is estimated at roughly $5.9B in 2025 growing to $24.4B by 2033, while the much narrower "world model simulators" segment specifically is put at just $127M in 2025 rising to about $3.1B by 2036 — a wide analyst spread that underscores how early and undefined this category still is.
- **Moat:** the founding team's run at Nvidia's Spatial Intelligence Lab building world models for autonomous vehicles and humanoid agents is a genuine technical head start, but be skeptical — Nvidia itself, DeepMind and other well-funded labs are chasing the same category, so today the moat is talent and compute access, not a proprietary dataset.

## AI-native challengers are rebuilding licensed professional work from scratch, not bolting AI onto it

The first wave of AI in regulated professional services mostly meant a copilot bolted onto existing software — a drafting assistant inside the audit tool, a research summarizer inside the portfolio system. This week's smaller bets took the opposite approach: start a new, fully licensed firm and redesign the entire workflow around AI from day one, with the human professional kept in the loop only where liability actually requires their judgment.

That's only viable now because large language models have gotten reliable enough to handle structured, auditable work — reconciliations, transaction analysis, systematic research — when a licensed human still signs off at the end, which is exactly the arrangement regulators in both audit and asset management already require. The opportunity isn't a smarter model; it's that nobody has redesigned the underlying operating model around what AI can now do, because the incumbents built their businesses around the manual version and have little incentive to gut it.

Who wins if the approach holds: new entrants that can move as fast as a software startup while still clearing the same licensing and liability bar as incumbents — a combination most software-only challengers to professional services have never had. Who's exposed: legacy vendors selling seat-based tools to the same professionals, since a firm that redesigns the workflow itself needs far fewer of those seats. Both bets below are still pre-launch or in early pilots, so neither has proven it can hold up at the caseload and asset volumes that make the underlying markets big in the first place.

### [Repodo](https://www.repodo.com/) — €8.2M pre-seed (led by Hedosophia and Seed Capital)
- **Problem:** audit is a mandated, licensed function still run largely on manual, spreadsheet-driven work — data collection, reconciliations, documentation, transaction analysis — that doesn't scale with client count and gets slower and more expensive as firms take on more clients.
- **Product:** an AI-native audit firm, not software sold to auditors, that automates data collection, reconciliation, documentation and transaction analysis while licensed auditors retain risk assessment, professional judgment and final sign-off.
- **Customer:** small and medium-sized businesses in Denmark first, with European expansion planned — the segment currently underserved by Big-Four-style audit pricing and turnaround times.
- **Stage:** €8.2M pre-seed (roughly $9.5M), the company's first raise, led by Hedosophia and Seed Capital; founded by former Lunar executives Ken Villum Klausen, Peter Andreasen and Joachim Strøjer Hansen alongside qualified auditor Anders Houmann, still pre-launch with no clients disclosed yet ([announcement](https://www.repodo.com/press/raises-pre-seed-funding)).
- **TAM:** Europe's audit market is worth more than €80B annually according to the company, though that spans firms of every size and audit tier — Repodo's actual addressable slice, the SMB segment it's starting with, is a fraction of that headline figure.
- **Moat:** pairing fintech-building speed (the Lunar pedigree) with an actual licensed auditor co-founder is a rare combination in a profession that's hard to enter without both — but audit is liability-heavy and trust-gated, so the real test is winning the same professional-indemnity confidence incumbents already have, one client relationship at a time.

### [Itoflow](https://itoflow.ai/) — $2.5M pre-seed (led by Balderton Capital)
- **Problem:** small and mid-sized investment firms — hedge funds, family offices — want the research coverage and portfolio monitoring of a large in-house quant team without the cost of hiring and running one.
- **Product:** AI agents that learn a firm's own investment approach, risk constraints and review criteria described in plain English, then run continuous quantitative research, backtesting and portfolio monitoring against that mandate across equities, bonds, ETFs, commodities and digital assets.
- **Customer:** hedge funds and family offices that can't justify building an internal quantitative research function but want institutional-grade research and monitoring coverage.
- **Stage:** $2.5M pre-seed led by Balderton Capital, with angel participation from Cleo founder Barney Hussey-Yeo; founded by CEO Aditya Jha, a nine-year quant researcher at JPMorgan, RBC and Tower Research Capital, currently in paid pilots with a number of hedge funds and family offices per the company, with no named customers or revenue disclosed ([announcement](https://www.balderton.com/news/itoflow-raises-2-5-million-to-bring-institutional-grade-portfolio-management-to-every-investment-firm/)).
- **TAM:** portfolio management software estimates range from roughly $3.7–8.9B in 2026 up to $7–33B by the early 2030s depending on the analyst and scope — a wide enough spread that any single figure should be read as directional rather than precise.
- **Moat:** the founder's quant-trading background paired with infrastructure engineers from Apple, Google and Microsoft is a credible team, but the gating factor for a firm handing real portfolio decisions to an outside agent is trust and track record built over time, not the underlying technology.

## What to watch next week

Callosum's $100M seed is its second in six months after a $10.25M round in February — worth watching whether the jump reflects real customer traction with its Tailored Inference API or mostly the UK Sovereign AI Fund's need to make a first, headline-grabbing investment. And with three companies now chasing the same "world model" category as Veeda — Nvidia and DeepMind among them — the more interesting story next quarter may be which of them ships a model good enough that a robotics company actually trains on it, rather than who raised the biggest round to build one.
