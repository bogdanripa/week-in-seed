# The Week in Seed: The Biggest Checks Went to Keeping AI Safe and Powered

### Two funds wrote seed checks above $50M to police AI's own supply chain and re-engineer the data centers behind it, while smaller rounds bet that autonomous agents — including ones built for U.S. intelligence work — are ready to act without asking first.

This week's money mostly skipped the product a person would ever touch. It went underneath — into the plumbing that keeps AI itself safe and running, and into agents trusted to act on their own once a human only reviews the exceptions.

## This week's two largest seed checks funded AI's own supply chain, not a product people use

This week's two largest seed rounds didn't fund anything a person will ever click on. One polices what an enterprise's AI agents are allowed to install and do; the other re-engineers the physical plant — power, cooling, compute — that AI factories run on. Neither company sells to end users. Both sell to the industry that's building AI itself.

That's a rational response to how fast the agent ecosystem has sprawled and how far AI's physical footprint has outgrown the tools built to design it. Enterprises are wiring untrusted skills, plugins and MCP servers into production agent fleets faster than any security team can review them by hand. Meanwhile the multiphysics problems behind a new AI data center — power delivery, cooling, network routing — that used to take engineering teams months are now the bottleneck on how fast new AI capacity can come online. Both problems trace back to the same fact: AI adoption is outrunning the infrastructure built to make it safe and buildable.

That also explains why the checks are sized the way they are. A round this large only makes sense if a category is being priced for winner-take-most economics before a Series A can even happen — funds aren't paying for revenue here (one of the two companies is still pre-product), they're paying for position in a market they expect to consolidate around one or two default vendors. Who wins if that holds: whichever vendor becomes the layer every other AI company has to route through. Who's exposed: the point solutions and internal tooling that AI labs and enterprises have been building themselves, which now have to compete with venture-scale capital aimed at owning the category outright.

### [Physical Superintelligence](https://psi.inc/) — $58M seed (led by Breakthrough Energy Ventures)
- **Problem:** the multiphysics engineering behind new AI data centers — power, cooling, network and compute design — is too slow and too human-bottlenecked to keep pace with how fast AI capacity needs to come online, and fundamental physics research itself is bottlenecked by the same limited supply of expert researcher-hours.
- **Product:** "Emmy," a platform of virtual physicists running on a proprietary reasoning engine paired with a large, curated library of simulations; the first commercial product applies Emmy to design and retrofit AI data centers, solving coupled power/cooling/network/compute problems before or after construction. The company is also open-sourcing "Get Physics Done," a research copilot for physicists, and is a technical partner on the Fermi Explorer interstellar-probe project.
- **Customer:** hyperscalers, AI labs and data-center developers who need to solve multiphysics design problems for new or existing AI facilities, plus (via the open-source copilot) academic physicists.
- **Stage:** $58M seed — the company's first raise, pre-revenue with no disclosed paying customers yet — led by Breakthrough Energy Ventures, with Dragon Global, Robot Ventures, Solari, Susa, Ron Conway's SV Angel, Valkyrie, Balaji Srinivasan and Anthony Scaramucci, plus individual investors from OpenAI, Nvidia, SoftBank Energy, Oracle, Hugging Face, Jump Capital and the a16z Scout Fund; the company says the round lands above the 99th percentile of comparable enterprise-software seed deals ([announcement](https://www.unite.ai/physical-superintelligence-raises-58m-seed-round/)).
- **TAM:** physics-based engineering simulation software alone is estimated at roughly $15–17.5B in 2025, growing toward $42B by 2033–34 across several analyst estimates — before counting the far harder to size market for AI-driven physical-law discovery the company says it's ultimately chasing.
- **Moat:** an unusually dense team of physicists and AI researchers from OpenAI, Nvidia, Google, MIT, Oxford and the Institute for Advanced Study is a genuine head start on a hard problem, but "discover new laws of physics" is an extraordinary claim with zero product proof yet — the near-term test is whether data-center operators actually buy the multiphysics optimization tool, not whether Emmy ever produces a scientific breakthrough.

### [Air](https://air.security/) — $50M seed, in two tranches (led by Sequoia Capital, then Greenoaks)
- **Problem:** enterprises are plugging untrusted AI skills, plugins and MCP servers into production agent fleets faster than security teams can vet them by hand — Air's own research counts more than 17,800 public AI add-ons with 6.7M installs pulled from unverified sources, some impersonating Anthropic or OpenAI to run arbitrary code.
- **Product:** a "Context Firewall for AI Agents" — Control (fleet-wide agent governance), Filter (vets add-ons before install), Defend (protects runtime actions) and Marketplace (pre-vetted extensions) — that discovers every agent running inside an organization and continuously vets what it's allowed to use.
- **Customer:** enterprise security and platform teams rolling out AI agents at scale in regulated industries — financial services and pharma show the strongest early demand — who need a control layer in place before something goes wrong, not after.
- **Stage:** $50M seed across two rounds closed weeks apart — $10M led by Sequoia Capital, then $40M led by Greenoaks — with Swish, Netz and angels including Wiz co-founder Yinon Costica and Clay co-founder Varun Anand; the company already has 20+ paying customers, roughly a quarter of them large enterprises, six months after founding ([announcement](https://techcrunch.com/2026/09/01/air-raises-50m-to-help-companies-vet-the-skills-and-add-ons-ai-agents-use/)).
- **TAM:** estimates for AI-agent security vary widely by scope — from about $1.6B in 2026 growing to $13.5B by 2032 on the narrow definition, up to $18.7B growing past $500B by 2035 on the broadest one — a spread wide enough that any single figure should be read as directional only.
- **Moat:** two Unit 8200 offensive-security veterans building the defense their own training taught them to break is a credible origin story, and a live marketplace creates some switching cost once agents come to depend on it — but the underlying idea, vetting what plugs into an agent fleet, isn't hard to copy, so speed to becoming the default layer is the real race, not the technology.

## Autonomous agents earned exception-only authority this week — even inside US intelligence work

The smaller checks this week went somewhere more surprising than another copilot: into agents built to act on their own, with a human reviewing only the exceptions, in domains where getting it wrong isn't a bad email — it's a missed threat or a broken commitment. Two years ago "AI agent" mostly meant a chat window suggesting a task. This week it meant software that places the call and navigates the hold menu itself, or — further out on the risk curve — assumes a false identity and spends months undercover inside a criminal network, checking in with a human analyst only when it finds something worth escalating.

What changed isn't primarily model capability; it's the willingness of buyers, including some of the most risk-averse buyers there are, to accept exception-based oversight instead of step-by-step supervision. That shift is happening because the alternative was never really available. Criminal and espionage networks already coordinate across encrypted channels at a scale human investigators can't match one-for-one; corporate admin work scales linearly with headcount most companies don't want to add. An agent that interrupts a human only when a decision actually needs one is the only model built for either problem.

We flagged the shift from advisor to operator six weeks ago; this week pushes it a layer further, into the oversight model itself rather than just the task list. Who wins: agent builders that can prove reliability well enough for a buyer to hand over exception-only authority — the trust bar, not the underlying model, is now the product. Who's exposed: workflow-automation tools that still make a human click "approve" on every step, once buyers see what a fully autonomous version can do.

### [Catch](https://catchagent.ai/) — $5M seed (co-led by Entrée Capital and Pitango)
- **Problem:** executives lose a large share of their week to scheduling, inbox triage, travel logistics and phone-tree admin work that most "AI assistant" tools still require a human to configure and supervise step by step.
- **Product:** an agentic admin assistant that observes how an executive actually works and independently handles scheduling, email, travel and follow-ups — including outbound phone calls, navigating hold menus and IVR trees itself — across Gmail, Outlook, Slack, WhatsApp, iMessage and phone, with no workflow-building required.
- **Customer:** business leaders and executives who want admin work handled end-to-end rather than merely assisted — the buyer is the person delegating, not an ops team configuring automations.
- **Stage:** $5M seed co-led by Entrée Capital and Pitango, with Seedcamp and Factorial Capital — the company's first raise — publicly launched at $99/month on September 2, 2026 after a stealth period in which it scheduled 12,000+ meetings, delegated 20,000+ tasks and handled 200,000+ emails ([announcement](https://www.unite.ai/catch-emerges-from-stealth-with-5m-seed-to-build-an-autonomous-ai-executive-assistant/)).
- **TAM:** the broader intelligent virtual-assistant software market is estimated at roughly $26–38B in 2026, growing toward $100–330B by the early-to-mid 2030s depending on the analyst — Catch's actual slice, admin work specifically, is a fraction of that.
- **Moat:** pre-launch usage numbers are a real signal of product-market fit, but "AI executive assistant" is one of the most crowded categories in software; CEO Nir Sabato's prior seat as an investor at lead backer Entrée Capital is a useful head start on distribution, not a technical moat — retention once the novelty wears off is the actual test.

### [Aslan](https://aslanprotects.com/) — $20.8M seed (co-led by Khosla Ventures and XYZ Venture Capital)
- **Problem:** criminal, espionage and terror networks now coordinate at internet scale across encrypted channels — Discord, Telegram, dark-web forums — faster than human investigators at intelligence and law-enforcement agencies can track them.
- **Product:** human-supervised, autonomous AI "operatives" that assume online personas and infiltrate hostile digital environments at scale, surfacing networks and leads for agency analysts to act on.
- **Customer:** components of the US intelligence community, the FBI, Homeland Security Investigations, and military commands including NORTHCOM — agencies that need infiltration coverage at a scale no human undercover program can match.
- **Stage:** $20.8M seed co-led by Khosla Ventures and XYZ Venture Capital, with BoxGroup, 2048 Ventures, Liquid 2 Ventures and Alumni Ventures; roughly 100 agents are already deployed, credited — while the company was still in stealth — with mapping a US-Mexico smuggling network, exposing a sanctions-evading fraud marketplace, and identifying a Chinese government tech-transfer and espionage effort ([announcement](https://www.tectonicdefense.com/aslan-emerges-with-20m-and-a-bunch-of-ai-agent-spies/)).
- **TAM:** government and defense AI software spending is put at roughly $15.6B in 2025 growing to $54B by 2033 on one estimate, with broader "AI in defense" forecasts ranging from about $8.5B to $25B in 2026 alone — Aslan's actual niche, agentic infiltration for intelligence work, is a sliver of that and isn't separately sized by analysts.
- **Moat:** signed intelligence-community and military contracts before a public launch is a genuinely hard moat to replicate — agency trust and clearance take years to build — but the same properties that make the product powerful, autonomous personas operating at scale with minimal human review, are exactly what will draw the most regulatory and civil-liberties scrutiny as it grows.

## A real drone incident, not a pitch deck, made the case for this week's counter-drone round

A single incident did more to justify a funding round this week than months of a pitch deck could have: a drone incursion shut down a major European airport the same week a laser counter-drone company closed its seed round, and the announcement was built explicitly around the incident. It's the physical-security version of a pattern showing up across AI infrastructure too — capital follows the moment a category's threat model stops being hypothetical.

The underlying pressure is real and building faster than most defenses can keep up: cheap consumer and military-surplus drones have turned airspace incursions over airports, bases and energy sites into a near-weekly occurrence in parts of Europe, and most existing countermeasures are either short-range, prone to collateral damage — kinetic interceptors, jamming that also disables civilian systems — or too slow to react at the volumes a coordinated swarm could bring. A defense that can track and disable dozens of targets a minute without putting a projectile in the air solves a problem kinetic and RF-based systems structurally can't.

Who wins if the pattern holds: vendors that get certified and deployed at government sites fastest, since procurement cycles — not the underlying physics — will decide who becomes the default. Who's exposed: legacy kinetic and jamming-based counter-drone vendors whose approach doesn't scale to swarm-density threats, and any newer laser entrant without a government relationship already in motion.

### [INLEAP Photonics](https://inleap-photonics.com/) — €20M seed (led by UVC Partners)
- **Problem:** drone incursions over airports, military bases and energy infrastructure are accelerating faster than most defenses can respond, and existing counter-drone options are either short-range or carry real collateral-damage risk.
- **Product:** eye-safe, ultra-fast laser beam-steering systems — Fastlight SHIELD and Fastlight HELD — that track and disable drones at up to 3km range, with the company claiming the ability to neutralize as many as 300 drones a minute.
- **Customer:** militaries, airports and critical-infrastructure operators (energy facilities, data centers) that need to stop drone incursions without ballistic risk to nearby people or property.
- **Stage:** €20M seed (~$22M) — the company's first disclosed raise — led by UVC Partners, with Germany's HTGF, Poland's Balnord, Estonia's Sentris and Denmark's Scale Capital; already working with Germany's Federal Ministry of Defence and the Bundeswehr Cyber Innovation Hub, and in a strategic partnership with defense contractor STARK Defence, with the CEO citing "strong order intake" ([announcement](https://tech.eu/2026/09/01/inleap-photonics-raises-eur20m-to-expand-laser-counter-drone-systems/)).
- **TAM:** counter-UAS market estimates vary enormously by scope — from roughly $2.3–14.4B in 2026 to $6–24B by 2030 across different research firms — a spread that reflects how loosely "counter-drone" is still defined as a category, though every estimate points the same direction: up, sharply.
- **Moat:** a Laser Zentrum Hannover spin-off pedigree gives INLEAP real photonics depth, and government relationships — the Bundeswehr, a signed defense-industry partner — are hard to replicate quickly, but laser counter-drone is drawing well-funded competitors globally, so the moat is whoever gets certified and deployed across the most live sites first, not the optics alone.

## What to watch next week

Air's roadmap leans hard on its Marketplace becoming the default distribution channel for vetted AI skills — worth watching whether any of its 20+ customers actually route procurement through it, rather than using Air purely as a filter bolted onto their own agent stack. And Aslan's model — autonomous personas operating inside encrypted criminal and state-actor networks, with a human in the loop only for exceptions — is exactly the kind of program that draws congressional and civil-liberties scrutiny once it's public; the pitch that raised $20.8M in stealth may get a very different reception now that it isn't.
