# The Week in Seed: Someone Has to Cool AI Down and Keep Its Lights On

### This week's smallest checks went to the heat and grid strain the AI boom is creating for itself, while the bigger money defended against hackers and, in India, hostile drones.

It was a week where seed money mostly played defense — against hackers, against hostile drones, and against the AI buildout's own physical exhaust. The checks that grabbed headlines were the ones fighting other AI; the ones worth watching were the two quiet bets on the heat and electricity the boom is generating for itself.

## Seed money is starting to pay AI's own utility bill

Every AI capacity story eventually runs into two walls that have nothing to do with model quality: chips throttle when they get too hot, and data centers can't get power fast enough to run them. Both constraints have been treated as someone else's problem — the chipmaker's, the utility's — for long enough that dedicated seed-stage bets on either one are still rare. This week had two, on opposite ends of the same pipe.

The chip-heat bet is upstream and abstract: better thermal materials mean AI hardware makers can pack more compute into the same rack without melting it, which is a licensing business, not a hardware business. The grid-strain bet is downstream and concrete: a smarter household water heater that can shed its own load when the grid is tight, sold to homeowners one unit at a time. Neither company sells directly to an AI lab or hyperscaler, and neither pitches itself as an "AI infrastructure" company — but both are only fundable now because AI compute demand made heat and electricity scarce enough to be worth a dedicated seed check rather than a line item in someone else's roadmap.

The harder question for both is distribution speed. Materials licensing lives and dies on whether a chipmaker actually qualifies and ships the compound at volume — a multi-year process with no guaranteed outcome even for genuinely better chemistry. Grid-responsive hardware lives and dies on installer networks and utility rebate programs, which scale metro by metro, not overnight. Both are structurally slow businesses funded at AI-native speed; that mismatch is worth watching over the next few quarters, not just this one.

### [Discovered Materials](https://discoveredmaterials.com) — $9M seed (led by Lightspeed India Partners)
- **Problem:** AI chips generate enough heat that thermal limits, not just compute limits, now cap how densely data centers can pack hardware — and discovering better thermal-interface materials the old way is slow, manual trial-and-error chemistry.
- **Product:** AI-agent-driven materials discovery software, combining Anthropic's models with custom physics simulations, that generates and validates candidate materials computationally, then licenses the winners and the discovery process to chipmakers.
- **Customer:** semiconductor and GPU manufacturers hunting for new thermal and packaging materials to keep next-generation AI chips cool without a full chip redesign — the trigger is thermal throttling capping how much compute they can sell per rack.
- **Stage:** $9M seed led by Lightspeed India Partners, with Peak XV Partners and angels Paul Graham, Gokul Rajaram and Thariq Shihipar participating; founded by Stanford materials-science PhD Akash Ramdas and Advaith Sridhar (previously at Persona AI and Luma Labs), emerging post-Y Combinator with a published "Material Discovery Bench" benchmark ([announcement](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/10/discovered-materials-is-playing-ai-whack-a-mole-to-hunt-cooler-chips/)).
- **TAM:** the data-center liquid-cooling market alone runs roughly $6B–$8B in 2026, heading toward $15B–$18B by 2030 across estimates — Discovered Materials is chasing a narrower materials-licensing slice one step upstream of the cooling-hardware vendors who'd actually deploy what it finds.
- **Moat:** a materials-science PhD founder and a public benchmark are credible technical signals, but the company's own claim so far is that its materials "match" properties of what chipmakers already use, not that they beat it — the real test is whether any chipmaker licenses and ships a Discovered Materials compound at volume, which no case study yet confirms.

### [Reservoir](https://www.reservoirhome.com) — $8M seed (led by Asymmetric Capital Partners)
- **Problem:** water heating eats roughly 18% of a typical household's energy bill and is a leading cause of residential water damage, yet the installed base is dumb — no leak detection, no ability to shift load off the grid when utilities and AI-driven power demand strain capacity at peak hours.
- **Product:** a heat-pump water heater with predictive heating, built-in leak detection, and grid-responsive scheduling that shifts heating cycles to off-peak hours or in response to utility demand-response signals.
- **Customer:** homeowners replacing an aging or failed water heater who want lower bills and leak protection, sold via a direct install model starting in the Boston area; utilities are a secondary customer through demand-response participation.
- **Stage:** $8M seed led by Asymmetric Capital Partners, with Founder Collective and MCJ participating; founded by Luke Winston-Almanzar (former Chief Business Officer at Formlabs), Gabriel Parisi-Amon and Jake Felser, with roughly 100 units installed and a target of 1,000 by the end of next year ([announcement](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/12/reservoir-raises-8m-to-make-water-heaters-that-people-and-the-grid-will-actually-want/)).
- **TAM:** the smart, grid-interactive water heater market alone is about $2.6B–$3B today, projected to roughly $7.9B by 2033, inside a broader ~$32B–$46B total water-heater market — Reservoir's real bet is capturing utility demand-response revenue on top of the hardware sale, a second revenue line most of that headline figure doesn't count.
- **Moat:** hardware plus a direct-install relationship is defensible if it scales past one metro, but at roughly 100 units installed, Reservoir hasn't yet proven it's a channel play rather than a boutique regional installer — and incumbents like Rheem or A.O. Smith bolting grid-responsive firmware onto existing product lines is the more likely long-run threat than another startup.

## AI's arms race is finally funding the defense side, not just the offense

Most of the AI-security capital of the last two years has quietly rewarded the attacker's side of the equation: general-purpose models get better at finding exploits and writing phishing lures faster than defenders can retool around them. Defensive tooling mostly got left to incumbent SOC vendors bolting AI features onto detection stacks built for a slower threat landscape. That imbalance is now explicit and numerical, not just a vibe, and this week it drew the kind of check usually reserved for a Series A.

The case for treating this as structural rather than hype: attacking and defending are different-shaped problems for a language model. An attacker needs one working path in; a defender has to be right nearly all the time, across ambiguous signal, without drowning a human team in false positives. A foundation model purpose-built for the defender's harder, noisier job — trained to sift logs and audits for the one real signal in the haystack — is a narrower and more technically defensible bet than a generic "AI copilot for your SOC," which is presumably why it can draw a $60M seed check instead of the usual single-digit-million one. It's also a different bet than the AI-code-security angle this newsletter flagged three weeks ago with a much smaller pre-seed round: that one guards what AI-assisted developers ship, this one guards what a security team has to react to in real time.

Who wins if this holds: enterprises with too many disconnected security tools who can bolt on an agentic layer without ripping out their existing stack. Who's exposed: legacy SOC-platform vendors whose moat was aggregating signal, not acting on it — a job an autonomous agent can increasingly do for them.

### [Corma](https://corma.ai) — $60M seed (led by Sequoia Capital)
- **Problem:** in the company's own internal red-team simulations, general-purpose AI attackers succeeded roughly 88% of the time against enterprise defenses, while AI-assisted defenders caught only about 12% of the same attacks — a gap existing security tooling wasn't built to close.
- **Product:** a foundation model trained specifically for defensive cybersecurity — log analysis, audit review, anomaly correlation — deployed as autonomous AI agents that plug into a company's existing security stack and work end to end, with an on-prem deployment option for organizations that require it.
- **Customer:** security teams at large, regulated enterprises (the company cites Fortune 100 and Fortune 500 customers in healthcare, financial services, energy, critical infrastructure and retail) already running mature but overloaded SOC tooling — the trigger is watching AI-powered attacks outpace human analyst capacity.
- **Stage:** $60M seed led by Sequoia Capital, with Khosla Ventures and Coatue participating; founded in 2025 by CEO Alon Pluda with researchers from Google DeepMind and Israel's Unit 8200, deployed for six weeks at launch with the company reporting threat-response times down more than 94% and security coverage expanded 15-fold ([announcement](https://fortune.com/2026/08/10/exclusive-corma-raises-60-million-from-sequoia-for-ai-trained-to-defend-against-cyberattacks/)).
- **TAM:** AI-in-cybersecurity spending is estimated at roughly $25B–$44B in 2026 depending on the estimate, projected toward $93B–$213B by the early 2030s (Grand View Research, Fortune Business Insights) — large enough to support several winners, though model quality alone won't be the moat once other labs ship comparable security-tuned agents.
- **Moat:** research pedigree (DeepMind, Unit 8200) and early Fortune 500 deployment data are real, compounding advantages if the agents keep improving with use — but the 94%/15x figures are self-reported from six weeks of deployment with no independent audit yet, and the underwriting question is whether "foundation model for defense" stays differentiated once Microsoft, CrowdStrike, or Sequoia's own portfolio ship a comparable agentic layer on top of models they already have.

## A drone interceptor got a pre-seed check before it had a disclosed check size

Defense tech overall has become a scale-up market — most of this year's record funding has landed in Series B-and-later rounds, with seed-stage deals a small fraction of total capital because hardware is expensive to build and defense procurement cycles run years, not quarters. Pre-seed hardware checks in the category are consequently rare, and rarer still outside the usual US cluster of Anduril-adjacent startups. This week produced one anyway, out of India.

The context matters more than the check size: counter-drone capability has gone from a niche defense-tech subcategory to a stated procurement priority across multiple militaries as drone warfare lessons from recent conflicts filter into planning documents, and India's own push for domestically built defense hardware gives an indigenous counter-drone startup a policy tailwind that a comparable US or European entrant wouldn't have. The underwriting question is whether that tailwind is enough to get a ten-month-old company with an undisclosed check through years of procurement and testing before a better-capitalized global player, or India's own defense manufacturers, close the gap.

### [KshatraLabs](https://www.kshatralabs.in) — pre-seed, amount not disclosed (led by Finvolve and India Accelerator)
- **Problem:** hostile drones and drone swarms are now a live threat to military and critical-infrastructure sites — airports, power plants, ports, refineries — and existing counter-drone systems are largely imported, expensive, and not built for GPS-denied or jammed environments.
- **Product:** HAWK, an autonomous interceptor drone that detects, tracks and neutralizes hostile UAVs at speeds above 300 km/h with under five seconds of launch readiness, running on a fully onboard edge-AI autonomy stack (APEX) so it keeps working without GPS or cloud connectivity.
- **Customer:** the Indian Armed Forces and operators of fixed critical-infrastructure sites who need to defend a perimeter against drone incursions — the trigger is a live incident or the broader wave of drone-warfare lessons pushing procurement forward.
- **Stage:** pre-seed led by Finvolve and India Accelerator, amount not disclosed; founded October 2025 in Bengaluru by CEO Rishav Dev Mishra, with hardware-in-the-loop testing completed at 90 m/s and active engagement with the Indian Armed Forces ([announcement](https://startuptalky.com/news/finvolve-backs-kshatralabs-in-pre-seed-round/)).
- **TAM:** global counter-UAS spending is estimated at roughly $2B–$13B in 2026 depending on scope, heading toward $10B–$24B by 2030 (MarketsandMarkets, Unmanned Airspace) — India's own indigenous-defense procurement push is a separate, harder-to-size tailwind not captured in that global figure.
- **Moat:** hardware validated at 90 m/s and a live relationship with the Indian Armed Forces are genuine early proof points in a category where most competitors are still slideware — but a ten-month-old startup with an undisclosed pre-seed check has years of procurement and testing ahead of it, against both global counter-drone players and India's own defense PSUs, that this round alone doesn't fund.

## What to watch next week

U.S. grid operators face a late-August FERC deadline to file revised interconnection tariffs — worth watching whether it speeds up or further clogs the queue that "bring your own power" data centers and grid-edge startups like Reservoir are both, in different ways, racing around. Also worth tracking: whether Corma's self-reported deployment numbers get any independent validation, and whether a second AI-native defensive-security lab lands a comparably large seed check — one $60M round is a data point, not yet a pattern.
