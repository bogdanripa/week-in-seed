# The Week in Seed: The Checks Went to Whoever Already Had a Way In

### A record African defense raise, an Australian seismic-sensor maker's move to the US, two healthcare AI bets on legacy data access over smarter models, and a cellular fix that skips new spectrum entirely.

It was a week light on new technology and heavy on access — to markets, to records, to spectrum, to geographies the bigger players had left alone. Nobody this week was pitching a smarter model; everybody was pitching a way in.

## Defense tech's frontier moved to where the primes don't go

The last two years of defense-tech seed money mostly recreated the US drone-and-autonomy stack in miniature: another quadcopter company, another AI targeting layer, competing for the same Western procurement budgets Anduril and Palantir already dominate. That market is getting crowded and the primes have a head start no seed check closes. The more interesting seed bets this week went around that fight instead of into it — building for threats and terrain the big vendors don't prioritize, then using that beachhead to expand into the US on their own terms.

The logic is straightforward once you see it: a defense company headquartered where the threat is — porous borders, resource theft, live weapons ranges — has a design and cost advantage a Bay Area startup replicating it from a slide deck doesn't. Local manufacturing sidesteps the export-control and lead-time problems that plague anyone shipping Western hardware into Africa or the Indo-Pacific. And a sensing modality the primes haven't standardized on — passive seismic instead of radar or electro-optical — is a genuine technical edge, not just a go-to-market one, because it doesn't emit anything an adversary can detect and jam.

Who wins if this holds: seed investors who backed the regional specialist before it had Western revenue, and who get a two-for-one — a defensible home market plus an eventual entry into the larger one. Who's exposed: generalist Western hardware startups whose main pitch was being first, in categories where "first" no longer means much once a cheaper, locally-manufactured or harder-to-detect alternative exists.

### [Terra Industries](https://www.terraindustries.co/) — $52M seed (led by 8VC)
- **Problem:** African nations defending infrastructure, mineral resources and borders have relied on imported Western, Russian or Chinese hardware with long lead times, export-control friction and no local manufacturing base — a supply chain mismatch that gets worse, not better, as threats to energy and mining assets escalate.
- **Product:** ArtemisOS, a software layer for real-time threat detection, autonomous mission planning and coordinated response, running across a hardware line of long- and mid-range autonomous drones (Archer VTOL, Iroko UAV), an autonomous ground vehicle (Duma) and an AI-powered sentry tower (Kallon).
- **Customer:** African governments and state-linked operators securing energy infrastructure, mineral resources, maritime assets and borders — the trigger is a live security gap Western suppliers are too slow or too export-restricted to fill.
- **Stage:** $52M seed (an $18M extension on top of an earlier close), led by 8VC, with Silent Ventures and Nova Global participating; founded 2024 by Nathan Nwachuku and Maxwell Maduka, now running two manufacturing sites in Nigeria and Ghana — the Ghana facility, opening Q4 2026, is billed as Africa's largest drone factory — with the company on track to book roughly $100M in contracts and tens of millions in revenue this year ([announcement](https://techcrunch.com/2026/08/17/terra-industries-closes-52m-seed-round-to-build-defense-infrastructure-for-the-global-south/)).
- **TAM:** Africa's unmanned defense vehicle market alone is estimated at $1.2B–$1.6B in 2026, growing 12–15% annually through 2035, inside a much larger Middle East and Africa defense market projected to hit roughly $109B by 2031 — Terra's addressable slice is still a small, contract-by-contract fraction of either figure.
- **Moat:** local manufacturing and a genuine Nigeria-to-Ghana production footprint are hard for an outside entrant to replicate quickly, and the contract pipeline is a real revenue signal most seed-stage hardware companies don't have — but "largest seed round by an African startup" is itself a fragile record, and the underwriting question is whether $100M in booked contracts converts to collected revenue at the pace African government procurement usually allows.

### [Seitec](https://www.seitec.com.au) — AUS$4M seed (led by ACTivate Capital)
- **Problem:** perimeter security, weapons-range safety and counter-drone detection have mostly relied on radar, cameras or acoustic sensors that either give away the defender's own position or struggle in cluttered, contested terrain.
- **Product:** two passive seismic-sensing systems — UXOTrackS, a distributed node array that detects, localizes and classifies unexploded-ordnance impacts on weapons ranges in near real time, and Nightingale, a perimeter-security and counter-UAS system that classifies personnel, vehicles and drones by their ground vibration signature without emitting anything detectable.
- **Customer:** military range operators and force-protection units needing range safety or perimeter monitoring without revealing their own sensor positions — the trigger is any environment where active sensing (radar, EO) is too visible or too easily jammed.
- **Stage:** AUS$4M seed first close led by ACTivate Capital, with Beaten Zone Venture Partners and UNSW Founders participating (an additional AUS$1M is still open in the round); founded 2021 by RAAF veteran Daniel Stevens and CTO Bevan Fairleigh, with UXOTrackS having already reached Australian Defence Force service entry and the new capital earmarked for US market entry ([announcement](https://www.startupdaily.net/topic/funding/canberra-defence-tech-startup-seitec-detects-4-million/)).
- **TAM:** the global UXO-detection market is roughly $4.35B in 2026 heading to about $8.1B by 2035, while the broader counter-UAS market it's now also chasing with Nightingale runs closer to $14.4B in 2026 toward $55B by 2034 — Seitec's passive-seismic niche is a sliver of either, not a challenger to the radar and EO incumbents that dominate both markets.
- **Moat:** an ADF service-entry milestone is a rare, credible validation most pre-revenue defense startups can't claim, and the passive/undetectable sensing angle is a real technical differentiator — but it's an unproven one outside Australian terrain and procurement relationships, and a AUS$4M first close (with AUS$1M still to raise) is a thin war chest for standing up a US go-to-market against better-funded incumbents.

## Healthcare AI's seed money went to the access layer, not the assistant

Healthcare has produced more AI pilots than almost any other vertical over the past two years, and most of them stall at the same wall: the model works in a demo, then hits an EHR system, a payer portal, or a patient's scattered history that wasn't built to be read by software. That access problem — not model quality — is what two very different seed checks this week were actually underwriting.

One approached it as infrastructure: build the API layer other healthcare AI companies plug into, so nobody else has to re-solve EHR and payer integration from scratch. The other approached it as a vertical product: build directly for one clinical specialty, aggregating a patient's scattered history — notes, recordings, past accounts — into something a clinician can actually use in the room. Both bets assume the same thing: that healthcare AI's bottleneck has shifted from "can the model do the task" to "can the system see enough of the patient to do it well," and that whoever solves the seeing problem captures value regardless of which model ends up winning underneath.

The risk in both cases is the same kind of risk: integration businesses live or die on how many systems they cover and how fast they add new ones, and clinical products live or die on whether a time-strapped, liability-averse clinician actually trusts what the software surfaces. Neither is proven by a pre-seed check — both are proven by adoption numbers a year from now.

### [Tross](https://ontross.com/) — pre-seed, amount not disclosed (led by All In Capital)
- **Problem:** healthcare AI companies building scheduling, referral, claims or documentation tools each have to independently solve months of integration work against fragmented, legacy EHR and payer-portal systems before they can onboard a single customer.
- **Product:** an API and integration layer purpose-built for AI companies, giving them production-ready connections into EHRs and payer portals for claim-status tracking, scribe-note uploads, appointment scheduling and similar workflows, without each customer rebuilding the plumbing themselves.
- **Customer:** healthcare AI startups and vendors — not clinics directly — who need EHR and payer connectivity to ship their own product, with US market entry as the immediate trigger for the round.
- **Stage:** pre-seed led by All In Capital, with DeVC participating, amount not disclosed; founded by Padam Kataria and Meet Shah, already supporting healthcare AI companies in the US market and on track to enable more than 200,000 patient interactions in 2026 ([announcement](https://www.indianstartuptimes.com/investment/tross-raises-pre-seed-funding-led-by-all-in-capital-to-scale-ai-healthcare-infrastructure/)).
- **TAM:** Tross sells into the AI-agents-in-healthcare market, estimated at roughly $1.1B in 2025 growing to $6.9B by 2030 at a 44% annual clip — a fast-growing but still-forming market where Tross is a picks-and-shovels layer underneath, not a participant competing for that revenue directly.
- **Moat:** being the integration layer other AI companies build on is a genuine network effect if it takes hold — more customers means more EHR/payer connections already built, which attracts the next customer — but with an undisclosed pre-seed check and no named EHR or payer partnerships yet, that flywheel is still a thesis, and a large EHR vendor opening its own AI-friendly API would undercut the whole premise.

### [Aisel Health](https://www.aisel.co) — €1.7M pre-seed (led by Caesar Ventures)
- **Problem:** psychiatrists spend more time hunting through referrals, prior notes and multiple disconnected systems to reconstruct a patient's history than they spend on the clinical judgment that history is supposed to inform — a capacity problem that AI scribes, which only speed up note-writing, don't touch.
- **Product:** a clinical operating system for psychiatry that turns interview recordings, existing documentation and patient-reported history into structured, contextual insight surfaced to the clinician at the moment it's needed, rather than a transcript the clinician still has to read and organize themselves.
- **Customer:** psychiatric clinics and providers — the pilot includes a child psychiatry practice doing ADHD diagnostics — where the buying trigger is clinician time lost to record-hunting rather than to documentation itself.
- **Stage:** €1.7M pre-seed led by Caesar Ventures, with Nordic Web Ventures, LifeX and Angel Invest participating alongside returning backers Rockstart and EIFO; founded 2024 by Augusta Klingsten Peytz and Christian Houen, currently piloting and blind-benchmarking the product against existing clinic workflows ahead of a planned seed round and UK market entry ([announcement](https://tech.eu/2026/08/19/beyond-ai-scribes-aisel-raises-eur17m-to-tackle-psychiatrys-capacity-crisis/)).
- **TAM:** mental-health software estimates range widely by analyst — from roughly $6.8B to $16B in 2026 depending on scope — with the narrower psychiatry-EMR segment alone put at over $20B; Aisel's actual addressable market is a fraction of any of those figures until it's sold outside pilot clinics.
- **Moat:** going deep on one specialty (psychiatry, not general clinical documentation) is a defensible wedge against horizontal AI-scribe companies with a wider but shallower feature set — but the moat is unproven at pilot stage, and the real underwriting question is whether "operating system for psychiatry" stays differentiated once a well-capitalized general scribe vendor adds a psychiatry-specific mode.

## Enterprise connectivity found a workaround instead of a rebuild

Enterprise indoor cellular has had the same fix for two decades: a Distributed Antenna System, built and tuned per carrier, that costs real money and takes months to years to install. It's a known, expensive, slow-moving market — which is exactly the kind of category seed investors usually skip in favor of something newer. This week's bet on it wasn't a new radio technology; it was a business-model workaround using spectrum and infrastructure that already exists.

The idea: instead of building a separate system for each mobile carrier, share CBRS spectrum and cloud-managed infrastructure across all of them, so an enterprise gets multi-operator coverage from one deployment instead of several. That's a neutral-host model, not a technical breakthrough, and it only works because CBRS spectrum-sharing rules already exist and carriers are willing to plug into someone else's shared infrastructure rather than build their own in every building. It's a bet on regulatory and business-model timing, not on radio physics.

Who wins if the model holds: enterprises with the worst indoor coverage problems — healthcare, manufacturing, critical infrastructure — who need reliability for reasons well beyond convenience, including 911 compliance and, increasingly, connectivity for automated equipment. Who's at risk: legacy DAS integrators whose entire business model is the expensive, slow, single-carrier build the neutral-host approach is designed to undercut.

### [InfiniG](https://www.infinig.io/) — $5.2M seed (co-led by J2 Ventures and Stormbreaker Ventures)
- **Problem:** enterprises with poor indoor cellular coverage — dropped calls, dead zones, 911 non-compliance — have had one expensive answer for two decades: a per-carrier Distributed Antenna System that takes years to install and multiplies in cost with every additional operator.
- **Product:** Mobile Coverage as a Service (MCaaS), a cloud-managed neutral-host platform that shares CBRS spectrum across multiple mobile operators through a single deployment, working with any phone and subscription with no special SIM cards or apps required.
- **Customer:** enterprises and property owners — the company cites healthcare, retail, manufacturing, hospitality, education and critical infrastructure — for whom the trigger is a documented coverage failure (dropped calls, safety compliance, or, increasingly, unreliable connectivity for automated equipment) rather than a general upgrade cycle.
- **Stage:** $5.2M seed co-led by J2 Ventures and Stormbreaker Ventures; founded by CEO Joel Lindholm and a team of enterprise-cellular veterans from Meta, with three years already spent deploying MCaaS at large enterprises before this round ([announcement](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/infinig-secures-funding-to-modernize-enterprise-cellular-for-the-next-decade-302853188.html)).
- **TAM:** the enterprise neutral-host opportunity is estimated at roughly $1.3B by 2030 by ABI Research, sitting inside a broader neutral-host market of $2.8B in 2024 heading toward $7.8B by 2033, and a still-larger $10.9B-plus distributed-antenna-system market InfiniG is positioned to undercut on cost and install time.
- **Moat:** three years of enterprise deployment experience before raising this round is a real head start on the operational complexity of multi-operator integration — but the model depends on continued carrier cooperation and CBRS access that InfiniG doesn't control, and a carrier deciding to build its own neutral-host offering would remove the gap InfiniG is currently filling.

## What to watch next week

Terra Industries' Ghana facility is due to open in Q4 2026 — worth checking whether "Africa's largest drone factory" actually ships product on that timeline, since the company's contract pipeline assumes it does. Also worth tracking: whether Seitec closes the remaining AUS$1M of its round before or after any US contract news, and whether either healthcare integration bet — Tross's horizontal API play or Aisel's vertical clinical OS — is the one that shows real customer growth first, since they're effectively two different answers to the same underlying bet.
